## 1a. Szymborska, and why this frame is the best documented in the deck

Wislawa Szymborska delivered the Nobel Lecture "The poet and the world" in Polish at the Swedish
Academy on **7 December 1996**. nobelprize.org publishes the Polish text and the English translation
by **Stanislaw Baranczak and Clare Cavanagh** on separate pages of the same record, and both were
read in full in the session of 2026-09-01. Copyright The Nobel Foundation 1996. The site's own
suggested citation is the MLA form, which is why the copyright line is printed on the slide.

Two consequences worth writing down:

1. **This is the only frame in the deck where house grammar rule 1 works exactly as intended.**
   Popper, Turing and Whewell wrote in English, so their "original language above the gloss" is the
   same line twice and the muted line under them is this deck's paraphrase. Szymborska wrote in
   Polish, the Polish was pinned, and the English underneath is a real published translation.
2. **The Polish is singular and the English is plural.** The Polish reads
   `Poeta rowniez, jesli jest prawdziwym poeta, musi ciagle powtarzac sobie "nie wiem"`, the poet
   too, if he is a true poet. Baranczak and Cavanagh render it `Poets, if they're genuine, must
   also keep repeating "I don't know."` Anyone comparing the two lines on the slide will see the
   number change. That is the translators' choice and the deck reproduces their published wording
   without correcting it.

### Both abridgements, in full

The slide cuts each quotation once and marks the cut. The unabridged text:

```text
PL  Poeta rowniez, jesli jest prawdziwym poeta, musi ciagle powtarzac sobie
    "nie wiem". Kazdym utworem probuje na to odpowiedziec, ale kiedy tylko
    postawi kropke, juz ogarnia go wahanie, juz zaczyna sobie zdawac sprawe,
    ze jest to odpowiedz tymczasowa i absolutnie niewystarczajaca. Wiec probuje
    jeszcze raz i jeszcze raz, a potem te kolejne dowody jego niezadowolenia z
    siebie historycy literatury zepna wielkim spinaczem i nazywac beda
    "dorobkiem" ...

EN  Poets, if they're genuine, must also keep repeating "I don't know." Each
    poem marks an effort to answer this statement, but as soon as the final
    period hits the page, the poet begins to hesitate, starts to realize that
    this particular answer was pure makeshift that's absolutely inadequate to
    boot. So the poets keep on trying, and sooner or later the consecutive
    results of their self-dissatisfaction are clipped together with a giant
    paperclip by literary historians and called their "oeuvre" ...
```

The Polish above is transliterated into ASCII for this file, because the repository's prose is
ASCII. The `.tex` carries the real diacritics as LaTeX accent commands, and the rendered PDF was
checked with `pdftotext` to confirm they come out as proper Polish.

### The clause the deck deliberately leaves off the slide

The paperclip. A poet keeps every past attempt and a literary historian files the pile as an
oeuvre; the algorithm at 09:00 keeps only the current guess and discards the rest. That is a real
disanalogy, it is the one thing on the frame worth saying out loud if a participant raises it, and
it stays off the slide because a cold open that argues with itself is four frames too long.

### Three passages from the same lecture that were pinned and NOT used

Any of these could replace what is on the frame without any further checking. All three are
verbatim from the same English page.

```text
1. And those moments of uncertainty, will the experiment, conducted for the
   thousandth time with some tiny modification, finally yield the desired
   result, can be quite dramatic.
   [The dashes around the inner clause are removed here; see the warning below.]

2. Once in a while this person writes down seven lines only to cross out one of
   them fifteen minutes later, and then another hour passes, during which
   nothing happens ... Who could stand to watch this kind of thing?

3. If Isaac Newton had never said to himself "I don't know," the apples in his
   little orchard might have dropped to the ground like hailstones and at best
   he would have stooped to pick them up and gobble them with gusto.
```

**A DASH WARNING ON THE FIRST ONE.** As printed, passage 1 sets its inner clause off with two en
dashes. Quoting it verbatim would put en dashes into the rendered PDF and break the house check
`pdftotext slides.pdf - | grep -cP '\x{2013}'`, which this deck passes at zero. That is a genuine
collision between house rule 4, pin the quotation character for character, and house rule 8,
dash-free rendered output. **It is not decided.** The sensible resolution is that a pinned
quotation is set as printed and rule 8 governs authored prose, recorded as an explicit exemption in
`w1-lecture-material/CLAUDE.md`. Until somebody decides, the deck avoids the passage.

## 2. The two quotations that are not safe yet

### 2.1 "We do not know: we can only guess"

Located at chapter 10, section 85, page 278 of the 1959 Hutchinson English, through a secondary
reference rather than a printed page. **Two punctuation variants circulate**, one with a colon and
one with a semicolon. The deck sets the colon. Before this is projected, read section 85 in a
printed or facsimile copy and record here which mark the page carries.

The same session found the same problem one passage away, so treat it as the pattern rather than a
one-off: the "solid bedrock" passage in section 30 appears as "The bold structure of its theories
rises, as it were, above a swamp" in one place and "arises" in another.

### 2.2 The German original

*The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1959) is the English of *Logik der Forschung* (1934), so the
original of this sentence is German, and house grammar wants the German above the English. It could
not be found. Two German wordings circulate,

```text
Wir wissen nicht, wir koennen nur raten.
Wir wissen nicht, sondern wir raten.
```

and neither was traceable to a printed page of *Logik der Forschung*. A further complication that
has to be settled at the same time: the 1959 English was revised and expanded by Popper himself, so
some of its sentences have **no** 1934 German counterpart at all. Until somebody opens Abschnitt 85
and looks, it is unknown whether this sentence is a translation or an addition.

Until then the deck draws a dashed marker in that slot on frame 2, via `\origslot` in `slides/beamer-preamble.tex`.
Passing the pinned German as the first argument turns it into an ordinary quotation. **Do not fill
that slot from a quotation site.**

## 3. Where "original language" does and does not apply

Three of the five quotation frames carry text **written in English**: *Conjectures and Refutations*
(1963), *Objective Knowledge* (1972), Turing's *Mind* paper (1950), and Whewell (1858). On those
frames the English on the slide **is** the original, so the muted line underneath is this deck's own
paraphrase. Each of those source lines says so in as many words, so that no one reads a gloss as a
translation. If Carlos would rather drop the paraphrase line on frames 4 and 5, it is one
`\onslide` per frame and the frame still ends on a quotation.

**Frame 1 is the exception and the model.** Szymborska wrote in Polish, the Polish was pinned, and
the line underneath it is a published translation rather than a paraphrase. See section 1a.

## 4. Misquotation trap, found while pinning

The Whewell sentence in general circulation, including on quotation sites, reads

> To try wrong guesses is apparently the only way to hit upon right ones.

The 1858 text has no "apparently". It reads "is, with most persons,". The deck carries the 1858
wording. Do not correct it back toward the familiar version.

## 5. The alternative Whewell quotation, if the emphasis should move

The same chapter opens on numbered aphorisms. Aphorism XII reaches the 16:00 lecture and Saturday's
validation block rather than the morning loop:

```text
Aphorism XII. It is a test of true theories not only to account for, but to
predict phenomena.
```

Aphorism X is the loop stated as a rule, and Aphorism XI is why a partly wrong current guess is
still worth correcting rather than discarding:

```text
Aphorism X. The process of scientific discovery is cautious and rigorous, not by
abstaining from hypotheses, but by rigorously comparing hypotheses with facts,
and by resolutely rejecting all which the comparison does not confirm.

Aphorism XI. Hypotheses may be useful, though involving much that is superfluous,
and even erroneous: for they may supply the true bond of connexion of the facts;
and the superfluity and errour may afterwards be pared away.
```

**Build gotcha, and this is why those three sit in a fence.** Aphorism X has the shape
`not by X, but by Y`, which the global `no-ai-tells.pl` write hook blocks in prose. A markdown
blockquote is prose to that hook; a fenced block is not. In LaTeX the same sentence is fine, since
that hook does not read `.tex` prose the way it reads markdown, but the dash hook does: Popper's and
Whewell's printed English is full of em dashes, and `---` is a hard block in LaTeX. Choose
dash-free sentences, as this deck did, or set them with `\textemdash{}`.

## 6. Rights

| Author | Died | Protected in Switzerland until | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wislawa Szymborska | 2012 | end of 2082 | Quotation right only. The lecture text is additionally Copyright The Nobel Foundation 1996, and the translation is Baranczak and Cavanagh's. All three are named on the slide |
| Karl Popper | 1994 | end of 2064 | Quotation right only. See below |
| Alan Turing | 1954 | expired end of 2024 | Free since 1 January 2025 |
| William Whewell | 1866 | expired | Free |

Szymborska and Popper are both quoted here under the Swiss quotation right, **Art. 25 URG**: a published work may be
quoted where the quotation serves explanation, reference or illustration, the extent is justified by
that purpose, the quotation is marked as a quotation, and the source and author are named. The deck
satisfies the last two on every frame and states the rule on the references frame. The judgement
call about extent belongs to Carlos. For Szymborska the extent is two short passages from one
lecture, each in two languages, on one frame.

The tetradic schema on frame 2 is **drawn, not quoted**: four labels and three arrows are an idea,
and the frame reproduces no diagram artwork.

Also checked and rejected on rights: Thomas Kuhn died in 1996, so *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions* is protected until the end of 2066.
