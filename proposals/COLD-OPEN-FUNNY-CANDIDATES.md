# Cold open for CAS BMAI HS26, weekend 1: the funny menu

Same slot as `COLD-OPEN-CANDIDATES.md`, Friday 4 September 2026 at 08:00, inside "Intro and
onboarding". Same budget, about three minutes. Different register.

Two philosophical openings are already built and both are good: `initial-states/cold-open-weekend1/`
(Descartes' four precepts) and `initial-states/cold-open-weekend1-popper/` (Popper's tetradic
schema). This menu exists because a room of working professionals at 08:00 on a Friday is a
different physical object from a room at 11:00, and because several things already sitting in this
repository are funny on their own without anything being invented.

**Everything below uses material that exists today.** Where a candidate needs a new asset, it says
so on its own line.

---

## 0. What "funny" has to survive here

Five constraints, all real, none of them a reason to give up.

1. **The room is managers at 08:00.** Anything that needs a setup paragraph before the laugh is
   dead on arrival. This is the same rule as the philosophical menu's rule 5, "no plot summary".
2. **The joke has to do the lecture's work.** A gag that buys a laugh and then gets abandoned costs
   three minutes and teaches nothing. Every candidate below is scored on whether cutting it would
   also cut a piece of content.
3. **No punchline slide.** `lecture-slides/SKILL.md` bans the big centred line in the brand colour.
   A frame ends on its final reveal. A joke can live in the room, in the plate, in the timing of a
   click, or in an ordinary body-text line at the foot of a frame.
4. **Never at the audience's expense.** A joke about managers who cannot do arithmetic ends the
   weekend before it starts. Every candidate here puts the lecturer, the material, or the schedule
   in the target position.
5. **No red and green**, as everywhere else in the house, and ASCII punctuation in anything
   authored.

## 1. "You are my validation set"

**Rank: 1.** The strongest thing on this page, because the joke and the syllabus are the same object.

**The bit.** Carlos says he wrote two openings for this morning and could not choose. Frame 1 of the
Descartes deck goes up for ten seconds, then frame 1 of the Popper deck for ten seconds. Show of
hands. The winner is played in full, right there. Then one line, in ordinary body text: you have
just done model selection on a validation set of about thirty people, and on Saturday at eleven you
will find out why the winner will look better this morning than it deserves.

**What it uses.** Both built decks, exactly as they are. Zero new assets.

**Clock.** Ninety seconds of vote, then the chosen deck's own three minutes. Call it four and a half
total, which comes out of a sixty-minute administrative block.

**Why it lands.** It is self-deprecating, it hands the room agency in the first two minutes of a
four-weekend course, and it plants `hs26-bmai-w1-validation` on Friday morning so that Saturday's
lecture has a memory to attach to. The winner's selection bias is real and it is the exact content
of the Saturday 10:30 lecture.

**What kills it.** Three things. A tied vote, which needs a rehearsed answer ready. The losing deck
is spent, so Carlos gives up the option to use it in weekend 2. And it settles open decision 6 in
the root `CLAUDE.md` by delegating it to the room, which is a decision Carlos may want to keep.

**Mitigation.** Show frame 1 of each deck only, with one spoken sentence each, so the loser is not
burned and can still be recycled later in the season.

## 2. The answer is last year, off by one

**Rank: 2.** The cheapest laugh in the repository and it is arithmetically exact.

**The bit.** A box on the screen with `2026` written inside it, drawn the way Carlos drew it by
hand. What is the length of its side. Somebody says forty-five. Forty-five is a good guess:
`45^2 = 2025`. So the side of a 2026 box, guessed at 45, gives you last year. The miss is one. The
whole morning is about what you do with a miss of one.

**What it uses.** `bmai_w1_notes_first_order_optimization_p1.jpg`, or a clean redraw of its box.
Verified this session: `45^2 = 2025`, `2026 - 45^2 = 1`, `sqrt(2026) = 45.0111097397076`.

**Clock.** Forty seconds.

**Why it lands.** The joke is a fact. It also does three pieces of work at once: it introduces the
signed miss, it shows that a wrong answer can be nearly right, and it makes the year of the course
part of the mathematics. If Carlos ever teaches this in 2027, `45^2` misses by two, which is its own
small joke about drift.

**What kills it.** Only that the room may not volunteer 45. Plant it with a TA, or put 45 on the
slide as "the guess most people make" and let the arithmetic do the rest.

**Pairs with.** Candidate 5, as its round one. Also with candidate 3 as the arithmetic half of a
two-part opening.

## 3. Crash the lander in front of them

**Rank: 3.** The most physical option, and the only one where the lecturer visibly fails on purpose.

**The bit.** Open `Minimum Lander Exploration.html` on the projector, mission 1, "Manual Moon
Landing". Carlos flies it by hand and crashes it. He tries once more and crashes again, or lands
badly. Then: an experienced professional, fully briefed, with the controls in his hands. At two
o'clock this afternoon a rule you can write on one line will do this better than I just did, and it
will do it without ever seeing the ground.

**What it uses.** Carlos's own single-file HTML page, opened straight from `file://` with no
network. Nothing to build.

**Clock.** Sixty seconds, ninety if the second crash gets a laugh.

**Why it lands.** Slapstick with a thesis. It sets up mission 5, "Engaging Autopilot", which is
gradient descent, and mission 2, "Navigating the Fog", which is the whole reason a derivative beats
eyesight. It also announces on day one that the lecturer will be at the front of the room losing to
his own algorithms, which is the register this course wants.

**What kills it.** Rehearsal. A crash that takes four attempts stops being funny at the second one.
Carlos should practice until he can crash it on the first try and land it on the third, and stop
there.

**Standing tension.** The lander is single-file and the `course-viz` skill mandates the multi-file
layout, an unresolved item in the root `CLAUDE.md`. Using it in a cold open does not resolve that,
and putting it on a projector in front of thirty people makes resolving it more urgent.

## 4. The worst genie in fiction

**Rank: 4.** The best pure comedy on the page, and the material is already written.

**The bit.** The genie plate, and his terms read out the way a procurement officer reads a contract.
You are lost in the desert. You rub a lamp. A genie appears and offers you your freedom, subject to
one condition: you must solve

    Q = 1 + 1/Q

There is no closed form supplied. There is no computer. There is no formula. There is only
arithmetic. Beat. Then: this is the worst service-level agreement any of you will see this year, and
you are going to satisfy it before the coffee break.

**What it uses.** The AINIT deck's genie frame, deck pages 24 to 27, with the line quoted character
for character from the extract: "No computer, no formula, only arithmetic." The Maxfield Parrish
plate `slides/img/epigraph/genie-parrish.jpg` from `git_ainit/rl-arcade`.

**Clock.** Fifty seconds.

**Why it lands.** Managers own the contract joke completely. The implicit specification with no
closed form is exactly what a business requirement looks like, and saying so out loud in the first
three minutes buys credibility for everything abstract that follows.

**Optional second beat, twenty seconds.** Six rounds of arithmetic starting from 1 give 1.6153...,
and the exact answer is the golden ratio, 1.6180339887... So the number every designer in the room
has been putting on slides since 2010 falls out of six rounds of division in the dark. Verified this
session.

**What kills it.** Rights on the Parrish plate are unresolved, open item 7 in the root `CLAUDE.md`,
and there is no `IMAGE-SOURCES.txt` anywhere in the AINIT repository for it. The bit works with the
equation alone on a plain frame, so the plate is optional.

## 5. Higher, lower, and then how much

**Rank: 5.** The best icebreaker, and the riskiest on the clock.

**The bit.** Round one. Carlos has a number between 1 and 100 in mind. The room shouts guesses. He
answers only "higher" or "lower". Count the guesses out loud as they go. Round two, new number, same
game, except now he answers with the actual miss: "you are twelve too low". Round two finishes in a
third of the guesses, in front of everybody, with no explanation from the front of the room.

Then one line: the difference between those two rounds is the difference between the rule I crossed
out in my notes and the rule I put a box around.

**What it uses.** Nothing but a lecturer and a room. It also stages the real conflict recorded in
`docs/summaries/04`: printed deck slide 51 and handwritten page 30 give the name Robbins-Monro to
different rules, and the handwriting crosses out the sign-only rule and boxes the proportional one.

**Clock.** Two minutes if it goes well. Three or four if the room is slow, which is the danger.

**Why it lands.** It is a party game at eight in the morning, it gets thirty strangers making noise
together before any content arrives, and the punchline is the single most important distinction of
the entire Friday morning. Nobody who plays this forgets why the size of the miss matters.

**What kills it.** A shy room at 08:00 on day one, before anybody knows anybody. Plant the first two
guesses with TAs. Hard-stop after eight guesses in round one regardless.

## 6. The lesson plan is mostly dots

**Rank: 6.** Charming, honest, and it hands the exercise straight to the room.

**The bit.** Project the photograph of handwritten page 1 as it actually is: the box of area 2026,
the five column headers, one row filled with `theta = 45`, and every other cell literally three
dots. Wait. Then: this is my lesson plan for the next two hours. You will notice it is mostly dots.
Filling in the dots is the exercise, and you start at ten.

Optional second click, and it is the better version: scroll to the foot of the same page, where
Carlos underlined "TODO: Come up with a scaffold of games & guide them". That TODO is dated before
the course and is still open.

**What it uses.** `bmai_w1_notes_first_order_optimization_p1.jpg` unmodified.

**Clock.** Forty-five seconds.

**Why it lands.** It is a real artifact in the lecturer's own hand, it converts an unfinished plan
into an invitation, and it is the most direct possible statement that the participants produce the
content of the morning.

**What kills it.** Delivery. Read wrong, "my plan is mostly dots" sounds unprepared rather than
deliberate. The fix is to name the dots as the exercise in the same breath, with no pause between.
Do not use this one if Carlos would rather the TODO stayed private.

## 7. The AI keynote you are not getting

**Rank: 7.** A joke about the genre, which this audience has sat through many times.

**The bit.** Three fast frames, five seconds each, played completely straight: Prometheus bringing
fire, with a portentous one-liner. Icarus falling, with another. The Tower of Babel, with a third.
Then cut, with no transition, to a hand-drawn box with 2026 written inside it, and: that is every
artificial intelligence keynote since 2015. We are going to do arithmetic instead, and by half past
ten you will have derived something that has your name on it.

**What it uses.** Section 10 of `COLD-OPEN-CANDIDATES.md`, which rejects exactly these three by
name as keynote cliché. The joke is the house's own reject list, performed.

**New assets needed.** Three public domain plates, rights checked per the house rule, plus three
lines of portentous copy that Carlos writes himself. This is the only candidate on this page that
needs anything built.

**Clock.** Sixty seconds.

**Why it lands.** It sets the register of the whole four-weekend course in one cut: no mythology, no
inevitability, no awe. It also inoculates the room against the marketing they will hear everywhere
else this year, which is a real service to a management audience.

**What kills it.** It only works if the room has actually endured those keynotes, and it is the one
bit here that can read as smug. Keep the portentous lines short and the cut fast.

## 9. Two recommended combinations

**The zero-build opening, three minutes.** Candidate 2, the box of area 2026 and the off-by-one
year, forty seconds. Then candidate 5, higher-lower-then-how-much, two minutes, hard-stopped. Then
one sentence handing over to onboarding. Nothing has to be built, nothing has to be cleared for
rights, and the room has laughed twice and met the day's central distinction before 08:04.

**The one that also settles a decision, four and a half minutes.** Candidate 1, the validation-set
vote, then whichever philosophical deck wins, played in full. This is the option that keeps the
built material, uses the humour as a frame around it rather than instead of it, and gives Saturday's
validation lecture a callback that the room helped create.

**Composition, stated plainly.** Comedy and the philosophical deck can coexist in three
arrangements, and they are genuinely different products:

| Arrangement | Effect |
|---|---|
| Funny opening replaces the deck | Fastest, warmest, loses the argument the two built decks were made to carry |
| Funny beat first, then the deck | A laugh and then a turn to seriousness, which is a strong shape and costs about five minutes |
| Deck at 08:00, funny beat at the 10:00 handoff | Keeps the opening solemn and spends the comedy where energy actually sags, at the start of the exercise block |

## 10. Considered and rejected

- **An AI-generated course introduction, played as a gag.** The single most exhausted bit in
  technical teaching by 2026, and this audience has seen it. It also spends the course's credibility
  on its cheapest joke.
- **Any bit whose target is the audience.** Jokes about managers who cannot do algebra, about
  executives and spreadsheets, or about anybody's mathematics. The room contains people who are
  genuinely anxious about the mathematics on the timetable, and the entire course design assumes
  they stay in the room.
- **A live large language model failing at arithmetic.** Tempting, on-topic, and unreliable: it needs
  a network, it may succeed, and it puts the weekend's subject in the position of a punchline before
  anything has been taught.
- **A closing centred one-liner in the brand colour after any of these.** Banned by the house skill,
  and it is also what turns a joke that landed into a joke being explained.
- **Self-aware jokes about how long the CLAUDE.md files in this repository are.** Funny to exactly
  two people, and neither of them is enrolled.
